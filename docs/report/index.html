Introduction

Last-mile delivery — the final leg of a shipment from a distribution facility to the end consumer — is the most expensive and operationally complex segment of any urban supply chain. In a city like New York, where traffic density, limited loading zones, and building access constraints compound the challenge, the proximity of warehouse infrastructure to residential population is a critical determinant of delivery cost and service reliability.

This analysis asks a straightforward question: which NYC neighborhoods lack adequate warehouse coverage, and how many people live in those gaps?

To answer it, I combine 2020 Census population data at the census tract level with NYC’s own property tax lot database (PLUTO) to map industrial and warehouse facilities across the five boroughs. I then apply a 1-mile service radius — a conservative threshold chosen to reflect realistic urban delivery reach given NYC traffic and parking constraints — and identify tracts that fall entirely outside that coverage zone.

The 1-mile threshold is intentionally conservative. A 5-mile radius, standard in suburban logistics models, eliminates virtually all gaps in NYC due to the city’s industrial density. The tighter radius surfaces meaningful variation and reflects the operational reality that a warehouse 4 miles away in Manhattan traffic may be effectively unreachable for same-day delivery.

A note on the warehouse data: PLUTO classifies every NYC tax lot by land use code. This analysis filters for code 06 (Industrial & Manufacturing), which includes warehouses, distribution centers, and freight terminals. This is a broader category than warehouses alone — some lots may be light manufacturing or workshops. It represents the best publicly available proxy for logistics infrastructure without purchasing commercial real estate data.

Methodology

The analysis follows four steps:

1. Data acquisition. Census tract population and geometry are pulled from the 2020 Decennial Census via the tidycensus package. Borough boundaries come from the Census TIGER/Line files via tigris. Industrial lot locations are queried from the NYC PLUTO dataset via the Socrata JSON API, then converted to point geometry using the lot centroid coordinates. Median household income is pulled from the 2020 ACS 5-year estimates.

2. Coordinate reference system. All layers are projected to EPSG:2263 (New York State Plane, Long Island), which uses feet as its unit of measurement. This CRS is optimized for accuracy in the New York metro area and is the standard used by NYC government GIS data.

3. Buffer analysis. Each industrial lot centroid is buffered by 5,280 feet (1 mile). The resulting circles are dissolved into a single merged coverage polygon using st_union(). This merged polygon represents the total area of NYC within 1 mile of at least one industrial facility.

4. Gap identification. Each census tract is tested for intersection with the coverage polygon using st_intersects(). Tracts with no intersection are classified as “far from industrial land” — they have no industrial or warehouse facility within 1 mile of their boundaries.

The income analysis complicates the simple “rich neighborhoods push warehouses out” narrative. Manhattan’s gap tracts include Upper Manhattan and Washington Heights, which are mixed-income communities — not exclusively wealthy enclaves. The more accurate explanation is that Manhattan’s industrial gap is driven primarily by land economics and zoning, not neighborhood income. Warehouse and industrial uses cannot compete with office, residential, and retail for Manhattan land at any income level. The gap follows zoning boundaries more than wealth boundaries.

Discussion

Key Findings

Manhattan accounts for the largest share of population outside the 1-mile industrial radius (0 residents), which is initially counterintuitive given the borough’s density. The explanation lies in land use and zoning: Manhattan has very few industrial-zoned lots, and the ones that exist are concentrated in specific corridors like the West Side railyards and the Meatpacking District. Broad residential swaths in Upper Manhattan and Washington Heights sit far from any classified industrial facility — not because residents pushed industry out, but because industrial use cannot compete economically for Manhattan land.

The income analysis confirms this: Manhattan’s gap tracts are not uniformly wealthy. The pattern follows zoning boundaries more closely than income lines.

Queens and the Bronx show smaller absolute populations in the gap, reflecting their larger industrial footprints along the waterfront and major freight corridors.

Limitations

Land use proxy. PLUTO code 06 captures industrial lots broadly. A more precise analysis would use actual warehouse operating data from a commercial source (CoStar, CBRE) or cross-reference with business license data. Real last-mile operations in Manhattan often run from garages, retail backrooms, and microhubs classified as commercial or parking in PLUTO — meaning the true coverage may be better than this analysis shows.

Edge effect. This analysis counts only NYC-classified lots. Major distribution hubs across the Hudson in New Jersey (roughly 1 mile from west Manhattan) and just outside the city line in Nassau and Westchester are excluded. This inflates apparent gaps in western Manhattan, Staten Island, and eastern Queens.

The 1-mile cliff. A tract 1.1 miles from a lot is counted as a gap; one at 0.9 miles is not. A continuous distance-to-nearest-facility measure would be more informative than a binary served/unserved classification.

Proximity is not service. Being within 1 mile of an industrial lot does not mean being well-served. Delivery reach depends on street network connectivity, curb access, building entry, and operating hours — none of which are in this dataset.

No cost or feasibility. Identifying a gap area does not mean a micro-fulfillment center belongs there. Rent levels, zoning restrictions, and curb access make Manhattan above 96th Street a difficult build regardless of demand.

Supply Chain Implications

As a first-pass screen of where NYC’s industrial land is relative to its population, this analysis is useful. As a recommendation for where to site logistics infrastructure, it needs the additional context above. For a retailer or 3PL evaluating NYC market entry, the honest takeaway is: Manhattan’s last mile depends heavily on facilities outside the borough, and any gap-filling strategy needs road-network accessibility and real operating data, not just proximity to a tax lot classification.

The income analysis complicates the simple "rich neighborhoods push warehouses
out" narrative. Manhattan's gap tracts include Upper Manhattan and Washington
Heights, which are mixed-income communities — not exclusively wealthy enclaves.
The more accurate explanation is that **Manhattan's industrial gap is driven
primarily by land economics and zoning, not neighborhood income.** Warehouse
and industrial uses cannot compete with office, residential, and retail for
Manhattan land at any income level. The gap follows zoning boundaries more
than wealth boundaries.

## Discussion

### Key Findings

Manhattan accounts for the largest share of population outside the 1-mile
industrial radius (`r comma(borough_summary$population[borough_summary$borough == "Manhattan" & borough_summary$coverage == "Underserved" & !is.na(borough_summary$borough)])`
residents), which is initially counterintuitive given the borough's density.
The explanation lies in land use and zoning: Manhattan has very few
industrial-zoned lots, and the ones that exist are concentrated in specific
corridors like the West Side railyards and the Meatpacking District. Broad
residential swaths in Upper Manhattan and Washington Heights sit far from any
classified industrial facility — not because residents pushed industry out,
but because industrial use cannot compete economically for Manhattan land.

The income analysis confirms this: Manhattan's gap tracts are not uniformly
wealthy. The pattern follows zoning boundaries more closely than income lines.

Queens and the Bronx show smaller absolute populations in the gap, reflecting
their larger industrial footprints along the waterfront and major freight
corridors.

### Limitations

**Land use proxy.** PLUTO code `06` captures industrial lots broadly. A more
precise analysis would use actual warehouse operating data from a commercial
source (CoStar, CBRE) or cross-reference with business license data. Real
last-mile operations in Manhattan often run from garages, retail backrooms,
and microhubs classified as commercial or parking in PLUTO — meaning the
true coverage may be better than this analysis shows.

**Edge effect.** This analysis counts only NYC-classified lots. Major
distribution hubs across the Hudson in New Jersey (roughly 1 mile from west
Manhattan) and just outside the city line in Nassau and Westchester are
excluded. This inflates apparent gaps in western Manhattan, Staten Island,
and eastern Queens.

**The 1-mile cliff.** A tract 1.1 miles from a lot is counted as a gap; one
at 0.9 miles is not. A continuous distance-to-nearest-facility measure would
be more informative than a binary served/unserved classification.

**Proximity is not service.** Being within 1 mile of an industrial lot does
not mean being well-served. Delivery reach depends on street network
connectivity, curb access, building entry, and operating hours — none of
which are in this dataset.

**No cost or feasibility.** Identifying a gap area does not mean a
micro-fulfillment center belongs there. Rent levels, zoning restrictions,
and curb access make Manhattan above 96th Street a difficult build
regardless of demand.

### Supply Chain Implications

As a first-pass screen of where NYC's industrial land is relative to its
population, this analysis is useful. As a recommendation for where to site
logistics infrastructure, it needs the additional context above. For a
retailer or 3PL evaluating NYC market entry, the honest takeaway is:
**Manhattan's last mile depends heavily on facilities outside the borough**,
and any gap-filling strategy needs road-network accessibility and real
operating data, not just proximity to a tax lot classification.
