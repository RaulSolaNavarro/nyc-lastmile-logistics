Introduction

In June 2026, NorthPoint Development offered 4,500 households in Hazle Township, Pennsylvania $10,000 each – in cash – if they would support approval of a 1,300-acre data center in their community. Locals pushed back, calling it a bribe. The township had already rejected the project on zoning grounds and enacted a moratorium on data center construction. The median household income in the area is around $60,000.

That offer crystallizes a question that is now live in communities across the United States: when a large industrial facility is proposed near a residential area, how much should affected residents actually be compensated – and how would you even calculate the right number?

The AI data center buildout has made this question urgent. In Q2 2026 alone, community resistance blocked or delayed 45 data center projects worth $68 billion across the U.S., according to Data Center Watch via parameter.io. The Brookings Institution framed the backlash as a proxy for a broader question: who decides where AI infrastructure goes, and who bears its costs? Vox put it bluntly: “Americans don’t know how to fight AI. So they’re fighting data centers.”

Residents object for concrete reasons – noise, water use, power consumption, and the suspicion that nearby homes will lose value. That last concern is empirically grounded. A 2011 hedonic pricing study from the Netherlands (de Vor & de Groot, using data on 70,684 home sales) found that homes within 250 meters of industrial sites sold for roughly 15% less than comparable homes beyond 2,250 meters – a discount that fades to near zero by about 1.4 miles out. Research specifically on data centers is more mixed: a George Mason University analysis of 2023 Northern Virginia home sales found prices higher nearer data centers, but Northern Virginia is an atypical case (high-income tech corridor, data center industry already integrated into the local economy). In Mansfield, Georgia, residents near a Meta facility reported cooling-tower hum audible indoors with windows closed, with independent measurements at 60–65 dBA at the property line. Some said they were considering moving.

None of this research answers the compensation question directly, because it describes outcomes – what actually happened to property values in places that already have industrial land – not what should happen in places considering it. To get at that, you need to know what living near industrial land correlates with in terms of income and revealed preference, across many communities and many distance bands.

That is what this analysis provides, for New York City.

What This Project Started As

This report began as a logistics gap analysis. The original question was narrow: which NYC census tracts fall outside a 1-mile radius of any industrial lot, and how many residents live in those gaps? The spatial analysis answered that – 31 tracts, 61,426 residents, concentrated in outer Queens and Brooklyn.

When I added income data, the logistics framing became secondary. Median household income rises monotonically with distance from industrial land across every distance band. The income gap between tracts within 0.5 miles of industrial land and those beyond 0.5 miles is roughly $18,000 at the median – a pattern that is statistically robust but reflects sorting, not a directly measurable cost.

That finding connects to the NorthPoint / Hazle Township situation via the Netherlands property value research. If a home near the proposed facility is worth $200,000 and industrial proximity discounts it by 15%, that is a $30,000 loss in asset value – three times the NorthPoint offer. The NYC income gradient shows the same directional signal: people with more resources tend to live farther from industrial land. The $10,000 offer looks hard to justify against either benchmark, though the right number depends on what you are compensating for, at what distance, and for how long.

This report presents both the original spatial analysis and the income gradient finding, and frames both in the context of the current debate over industrial facility siting and community compensation.

What This Measures and What It Does Not

The spatial analysis uses NYC’s PLUTO property database. Land use code 06 maps to building classes E* (warehouses), F* (factories), and L* (loft buildings) per the NYC DCP PLUTO Data Dictionary. This is a broader category than distribution-oriented warehouses alone. Data centers are not currently classified under code 06 in PLUTO – they appear in various commercial and utility categories depending on their specific classification. The income gradient finding applies to existing NYC industrial land as classified by PLUTO, not to data centers specifically.

The analogy to data centers is directional, not direct. Industrial lots (warehouses, factories) and data centers share characteristics relevant to neighboring residents – noise, truck traffic, land area, visual impact – but differ in others (data centers have less truck traffic, more power infrastructure, and different noise profiles). The Netherlands property value research covers industrial sites broadly; whether its 15% discount applies to data centers specifically is unknown.

What this analysis does establish: across NYC’s 2,327 census tracts, higher income is consistently associated with greater distance from industrial land. That pattern is robust, city-wide, and cannot be explained away as a quirk of the 31 gap tracts alone.

The 1-mile proximity threshold used for the binary gap classification is intentionally tight. A 5-mile radius eliminates virtually all gaps in NYC. The tighter radius surfaces meaningful geographic variation across the boroughs and aligns with walkable proximity (roughly 20 minutes on foot).

Data note (updated October 7, 2026): An earlier version of this report (published October 4) showed 38 gap tracts and 169,223 residents, with 14 Manhattan gap tracts. Those numbers were wrong. The Socrata API returns results in pages, and the initial pull hit the default 1,000-row limit, returning an incomplete PLUTO industrial lot dataset. A corrected pull (no row limit) retrieved the full set. With complete data, Manhattan has near-complete industrial land coverage (only 1 gap tract), and the city-wide gap falls to 31 tracts and 61,426 residents. All numbers in this version reflect the corrected pull.

Methodology

The analysis follows four steps:

1. Data acquisition. Census tract population and geometry are pulled from the 2020 Decennial Census via the tidycensus package. Borough boundaries come from the Census TIGER/Line files via tigris. Industrial lot locations are queried from the NYC PLUTO dataset via the Socrata JSON API with no row limit, then converted to point geometry using the lot centroid coordinates. Median household income is pulled from the 2020 ACS 5-year estimates.

2. Coordinate reference system. All layers are projected to EPSG:2263 (New York State Plane, Long Island), which uses feet as its unit of measurement. This CRS is optimized for accuracy in the New York metro area and is the standard used by NYC government GIS data.

3. Buffer analysis. Each industrial lot centroid is buffered by 5,280 feet (1 mile). The resulting circles are dissolved into a single merged coverage polygon using st_union(). This merged polygon represents the total area of NYC within 1 mile of at least one industrial lot.

4. Gap identification. Each census tract is tested for intersection with the coverage polygon using st_intersects(). Tracts with no intersection are classified as “far from industrial land.” In addition, the straight-line distance from each tract centroid to its nearest industrial lot is computed using st_distance(), enabling continuous distance-band analysis beyond the binary classification.

Distance-Band Analysis

The binary gap classification (covered vs. not) masks variation in how far residents actually are from industrial land. This section reports the share of population within each distance band, by borough, and the median straight-line distance from tract centroid to the nearest industrial lot.

Two distance measures are in use here and they give different numbers intentionally. The borough table (below) classifies a tract as “covered” if any part of it intersects a 1-mile buffer – the most generous possible reading. The distance table here uses the tract centroid, which is stricter: a large tract whose center sits 1.1 miles away counts as a gap by centroid even if its edge is technically within range. The 0.7% city-wide figure comes from the buffer method; the centroid method shows about 6% of Queens residents and 12% of Staten Island residents more than a mile from industrial land.

The binary gap comparison (the 31 covered tracts vs. the rest) tells the same story: gap tracts median $101,461 vs. covered tracts $69,275. But the distance-band table is more informative because it shows the gradient holds continuously, not just at the 1-mile cutoff.

The data cannot distinguish between two explanations: affluent neighborhoods may have successfully excluded industrial uses through zoning, or those neighborhoods may simply sit at the outer edges of where the city’s industrial corridors historically developed. Separating geography from politics would require zoning history and land use change data over time. What the data does establish is that the income gradient is real, city-wide, and statistically robust.

Discussion

Key Findings

Queens has the largest gap population (32,622 residents across 13 tracts), followed by Brooklyn (19,809 across 11 tracts). Both sets of gap tracts sit at the outer edges of their boroughs, distant from the industrial corridors concentrated along the waterfront and freight routes closer to the urban core.

Manhattan has only one gap tract (GEOID 36061000100, Census Tract 1), with zero counted residents. Its centroid falls at approximately 40.695°N, 74.043°W, in the harbor southwest of the Battery – consistent with harbor islands (Governors Island, Ellis Island, or Liberty Island) that carry no permanent residents. Zero is most likely a true count, not a differential privacy artifact. Manhattan’s near-complete industrial land coverage otherwise reflects the density of lots in specific corridors (West Side railyards, Meatpacking District, Hudson Yards area).

Median household income rises monotonically with distance from industrial land across all four distance bands, from $63,708 within a quarter mile to $90,379 beyond a mile. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test confirms the gradient is statistically significant (p < 0.001) across 2,206 tracts with income data. The data cannot say whether this reflects deliberate zoning exclusion or simply the geography of where industrial corridors developed, but the pattern itself is robust. The Manhattan gap tract has no income data, since it has no counted population.

The distance-band analysis adds resolution beyond the binary gap classification. About 16% of New Yorkers (96.6% within a mile minus 80.4% within half a mile) live in the 0.5–1 mile band. Staten Island stands out with the longest median tract distance (0.50 miles, vs. 0.21–0.31 for the other four boroughs). Queens has a shorter median (0.27 miles) but a longer right tail: about 6% of Queens residents are more than a mile from industrial land by the centroid measure, the second-highest share after Staten Island.

Limitations

Broad land use category. According to the NYC DCP PLUTO Data Dictionary, land use code 06 maps to building classes E* (warehouses), F* (factories), and L* (loft buildings). Factories and lofts are included alongside distribution-oriented warehouses, and the category gives no indication of whether a lot hosts an active logistics operation. L* specifically includes converted loft buildings and self-storage facilities that generate little noise or truck traffic – meaningfully different from a large active warehouse. This analysis measures proximity to industrial land as a class – it does not distinguish a 500,000 sq ft fulfillment center from a converted loft building or an inactive factory floor. Including L* lots weakens the “industrial facility” proxy and likely understates the income gradient around genuinely heavy industrial uses. A more operationally precise analysis would require commercial real estate data (CoStar, CBRE) or cross-referencing with business license records to isolate active logistics facilities.

Industrial land is not the only freight infrastructure. Real last-mile delivery in dense neighborhoods often runs from microhubs, retail backrooms, and parking garages classified as commercial in PLUTO. The gaps measured here may overstate true freight access limitations for some neighborhoods.

Edge effect. This analysis counts only NYC-classified lots. Major distribution hubs in New Jersey (roughly 1 mile from west Manhattan) and just outside the city line in Nassau and Westchester are excluded. This inflates apparent gaps in western Manhattan, Staten Island, and eastern Queens. The effect is largest for Staten Island: the borough sits adjacent to major New Jersey logistics corridors in Elizabeth and Linden (across the Arthur Kill and Kill Van Kull), and its distance numbers would look materially different if those New Jersey lots were included.

The 1-mile threshold. A tract 1.1 miles from a lot is counted as a gap; one at 0.9 miles is not. The distance-band table partially addresses this by reporting continuous bands, but the binary classification in the maps and borough table still uses the fixed threshold.

Straight-line distance is not travel time. Proximity to industrial land says nothing about road network connectivity, traffic conditions, loading access, or building entry – the actual constraints that determine delivery reach in an urban environment.

What the Income Gradient Implies for Compensation

The core finding is that NYC census tracts closer to industrial land have lower median household incomes, and the gradient is continuous and statistically robust. This does not prove that industrial proximity causes lower incomes – the causal story runs in at least two directions. Industrial corridors may have developed where land was already cheap (lower-income areas). Or affluent neighborhoods may have used zoning to exclude industrial uses over time, making the correlation partly a product of political power. Distinguishing those explanations requires zoning history data this analysis does not include.

What the gradient does support is a revealed-preference reading: across 2,206 NYC tracts, proximity to industrial land is consistently associated with lower household income. People with more resources tend to live farther from industrial land. That is consistent with the Netherlands research finding a 15% property value discount within 250 meters of industrial sites. Both patterns point in the same direction: industrial proximity is something people pay to avoid, or are compensated for through lower housing costs.

Back to the $10,000 question. NorthPoint offered Hazle Township residents $10,000 – a one-time payment – to accept a 1,300-acre data center. The Netherlands study gives the clearest benchmark: homes within 250 meters of industrial sites sold for roughly 15% less. If a home near the facility is worth $200,000, that is a $30,000 loss in asset value – a one-time hit, three times the NorthPoint offer. The discount fades with distance, so homes within 250 meters bear the full 15% while homes closer to 1.4 miles out see almost nothing. A flat $10,000 to everyone ignores that geography entirely.

The NYC income gradient is a different signal – correlation rather than cost. Tracts within 0.25 miles of industrial land have a median income of $63,708; tracts beyond 1 mile average $90,379. That $26,671 gap reflects sorting (more affluent households can afford to live farther from industrial land) rather than a direct price tag. Still, the direction is consistent: industrial proximity is something people pay to avoid, or accept in exchange for lower housing costs. A one-time $10,000 payment is a thin rationale for waiving that for a generation-long facility.

None of this means developers should not build. It means the compensation question deserves an empirical basis rather than an arbitrary one. Relevant inputs would include: the distance from the facility to each household, local property values before and after comparable facilities opened nearby, the expected duration of impacts (noise, traffic, visual), and whether payments should be structured as one-time checks or ongoing annual compensation tied to the facility’s operating life.

Implications for the AI Data Center Debate

The current conflict over data center siting is partly a zoning fight, partly an AI anxiety fight, and partly a legitimate dispute over who pays for infrastructure that benefits a much larger population. In Q2 2026 alone, community resistance stalled $68 billion in data center projects across the U.S. TechCrunch reported in September 2026 that “everyone can find a reason to dislike data center construction” – from noise and water use to AI ethics to simple neighborhood character concerns.

The Brookings Institution framed it as a democratic accountability problem: if AI infrastructure is built wherever companies choose, with local governments as the only check, communities end up negotiating individually against well-resourced developers without any common framework for what fair compensation looks like.

NYC’s industrial land income gradient does not resolve that, but it offers one empirical anchor. A city with 80 years of industrial zoning history shows that people consistently sort away from industrial land when they can afford to. If data center developers want to change that sorting – to site facilities in areas where residents have the resources to object and the political influence to succeed – the offer needs to reflect what those residents are actually giving up.

Whether the comparison between NYC’s industrial lots and a Pennsylvania data center is close enough to matter is a judgment call. The land uses are different. The geography is different. But the core mechanism – a large industrial facility arrives near a residential area, generating noise, traffic, and visual impact – is similar enough that the income gradient finding is at minimum a reasonable order-of-magnitude reference, and significantly better than no empirical basis at all.

## Introduction

In June 2026, NorthPoint Development offered 4,500 households in Hazle
Township, Pennsylvania $10,000 each -- in cash -- if they would support
approval of a 1,300-acre data center in their community. Locals pushed back,
calling it a bribe. The township had already rejected the project on zoning
grounds and enacted a moratorium on data center construction. The median
household income in the area is around $60,000.

That offer crystallizes a question that is now live in communities across the
United States: **when a large industrial facility is proposed near a residential
area, how much should affected residents actually be compensated -- and how
would you even calculate the right number?**

The AI data center buildout has made this question urgent. In Q2 2026 alone,
community resistance blocked or delayed 45 data center projects worth $68
billion across the U.S., according to [Data Center Watch via
parameter.io](https://parameter.io/community-resistance-stalls-68-billion-worth-of-ai-data-center-developments/).
The [Brookings Institution](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/data-center-backlash-signals-a-fight-over-ai-power/)
framed the backlash as a proxy for a broader question: who decides where AI
infrastructure goes, and who bears its costs? Vox put it bluntly: *"Americans
don't know how to fight AI. So they're fighting data centers."*

Residents object for concrete reasons -- noise, water use, power consumption,
and the suspicion that nearby homes will lose value. That last concern is
empirically grounded. A [2011 hedonic pricing study from the
Netherlands](https://papers.tinbergen.nl/09035.pdf) (de Vor & de Groot, using
data on 70,684 home sales) found that homes within 250 meters of industrial
sites sold for roughly 15% less than comparable homes beyond 2,250 meters --
a discount that fades to near zero by about 1.4 miles out. Research
specifically on data centers is more mixed: a
[George Mason University analysis](https://stopohiodatacenters.org/data-center-property-value-ohio)
of 2023 Northern Virginia home sales found prices *higher* nearer data centers,
but Northern Virginia is an atypical case (high-income tech corridor, data
center industry already integrated into the local economy). In Mansfield,
Georgia, residents near a Meta facility reported cooling-tower hum audible
indoors with windows closed, with independent measurements at 60--65 dBA at
the property line. Some said they were considering moving.

None of this research answers the compensation question directly, because it
describes outcomes -- what actually happened to property values in places that
already have industrial land -- not what should happen in places considering
it. To get at that, you need to know what living near industrial land
correlates with in terms of income and revealed preference, across many
communities and many distance bands.

**That is what this analysis provides, for New York City.**

### What This Project Started As

This report began as a logistics gap analysis. The original question was
narrow: which NYC census tracts fall outside a 1-mile radius of any industrial
lot, and how many residents live in those gaps? The spatial analysis answered
that -- 31 tracts, 61,426 residents, concentrated in outer Queens and Brooklyn.

When I added income data, the logistics framing became secondary. **Median
household income rises monotonically with distance from industrial land across
every distance band.** The income gap between tracts within 0.5 miles of
industrial land and those beyond 0.5 miles is roughly $18,000 at the median
-- a pattern that is statistically robust but reflects sorting, not a
directly measurable cost.

That finding connects to the NorthPoint / Hazle Township situation via the
Netherlands property value research. If a home near the proposed facility
is worth $200,000 and industrial proximity discounts it by 15%, that is a
$30,000 loss in asset value -- three times the NorthPoint offer. The NYC
income gradient shows the same directional signal: people with more resources
tend to live farther from industrial land. The $10,000 offer looks hard to
justify against either benchmark, though the right number depends on what
you are compensating for, at what distance, and for how long.

This report presents both the original spatial analysis and the income gradient
finding, and frames both in the context of the current debate over industrial
facility siting and community compensation.

### What This Measures and What It Does Not

The spatial analysis uses NYC's PLUTO property database. Land use code 06
maps to building classes E* (warehouses), F* (factories), and L* (loft
buildings) per the NYC DCP PLUTO Data Dictionary. This is a broader category
than distribution-oriented warehouses alone. Data centers are not currently
classified under code 06 in PLUTO -- they appear in various commercial and
utility categories depending on their specific classification. The income
gradient finding applies to existing NYC industrial land as classified by
PLUTO, not to data centers specifically.

The analogy to data centers is directional, not direct. Industrial lots
(warehouses, factories) and data centers share characteristics relevant to
neighboring residents -- noise, truck traffic, land area, visual impact -- but
differ in others (data centers have less truck traffic, more power
infrastructure, and different noise profiles). The Netherlands property value
research covers industrial sites broadly; whether its 15% discount applies
to data centers specifically is unknown.

What this analysis does establish: across NYC's 2,327 census tracts, higher
income is consistently associated with greater distance from industrial land.
That pattern is robust, city-wide, and cannot be explained away as a quirk of
the 31 gap tracts alone.

The 1-mile proximity threshold used for the binary gap classification is
intentionally tight. A 5-mile radius eliminates virtually all gaps in NYC.
The tighter radius surfaces meaningful geographic variation across the boroughs
and aligns with walkable proximity (roughly 20 minutes on foot).

## Methodology

The analysis follows four steps:

**1. Data acquisition.** Census tract population and geometry are pulled from
the 2020 Decennial Census via the `tidycensus` package. Borough boundaries
come from the Census TIGER/Line files via `tigris`. Industrial lot locations
are queried from the NYC PLUTO dataset via the Socrata JSON API with no row
limit, then converted to point geometry using the lot centroid coordinates.
Median household income is pulled from the 2020 ACS 5-year estimates.

**2. Coordinate reference system.** All layers are projected to EPSG:2263
(New York State Plane, Long Island), which uses feet as its unit of
measurement. This CRS is optimized for accuracy in the New York metro area
and is the standard used by NYC government GIS data.

**3. Buffer analysis.** Each industrial lot centroid is buffered by 5,280
feet (1 mile). The resulting circles are dissolved into a single merged
coverage polygon using `st_union()`. This merged polygon represents the
total area of NYC within 1 mile of at least one industrial lot.

**4. Gap identification.** Each census tract is tested for intersection with
the coverage polygon using `st_intersects()`. Tracts with no intersection
are classified as "far from industrial land." In addition, the straight-line
distance from each tract centroid to its nearest industrial lot is computed
using `st_distance()`, enabling continuous distance-band analysis beyond the
binary classification.

## Discussion

### Key Findings

Queens has the largest gap population (32,622 residents across 13 tracts),
followed by Brooklyn (19,809 across 11 tracts). Both sets of gap tracts sit at
the outer edges of their boroughs, distant from the industrial corridors
concentrated along the waterfront and freight routes closer to the urban core.

Manhattan has only one gap tract (GEOID 36061000100, Census Tract 1), with
zero counted residents. Its centroid falls at approximately 40.695°N, 74.043°W,
in the harbor southwest of the Battery -- consistent with harbor islands
(Governors Island, Ellis Island, or Liberty Island) that carry no permanent
residents. Zero is most likely a true count, not a differential privacy
artifact. Manhattan's near-complete industrial land coverage otherwise reflects
the density of lots in specific corridors (West Side railyards, Meatpacking
District, Hudson Yards area).

Median household income rises monotonically with distance from industrial land
across all four distance bands, from $63,708 within a quarter mile to $90,379
beyond a mile. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test confirms the gradient is statistically
significant (p < 0.001) across 2,206 tracts with income data. The data cannot
say whether this reflects deliberate zoning exclusion or simply the geography
of where industrial corridors developed, but the pattern itself is robust. The
Manhattan gap tract has no income data, since it has no counted population.

The distance-band analysis adds resolution beyond the binary gap classification.
About 16% of New Yorkers (96.6% within a mile minus 80.4% within half a mile)
live in the 0.5--1 mile band. Staten Island stands out with the longest median
tract distance (0.50 miles, vs. 0.21--0.31 for the other four boroughs).
Queens has a shorter median (0.27 miles) but a longer right tail: about 6% of
Queens residents are more than a mile from industrial land by the centroid
measure, the second-highest share after Staten Island.

### Limitations

**Broad land use category.** According to the NYC DCP PLUTO Data Dictionary,
land use code 06 maps to building classes E* (warehouses), F* (factories), and
L* (loft buildings). Factories and lofts are included alongside
distribution-oriented warehouses, and the category gives no indication of
whether a lot hosts an active logistics operation. L* specifically includes
converted loft buildings and self-storage facilities that generate little
noise or truck traffic -- meaningfully different from a large active warehouse.
This analysis measures proximity to industrial land as a class -- it does not
distinguish a 500,000 sq ft fulfillment center from a converted loft building
or an inactive factory floor. Including L* lots weakens the "industrial
facility" proxy and likely understates the income gradient around genuinely
heavy industrial uses. A more operationally precise analysis would require
commercial real estate data (CoStar, CBRE) or cross-referencing with business
license records to isolate active logistics facilities.

**Industrial land is not the only freight infrastructure.** Real last-mile
delivery in dense neighborhoods often runs from microhubs, retail backrooms,
and parking garages classified as commercial in PLUTO. The gaps measured here
may overstate true freight access limitations for some neighborhoods.

**Edge effect.** This analysis counts only NYC-classified lots. Major
distribution hubs in New Jersey (roughly 1 mile from west Manhattan) and just
outside the city line in Nassau and Westchester are excluded. This inflates
apparent gaps in western Manhattan, Staten Island, and eastern Queens. The
effect is largest for Staten Island: the borough sits adjacent to major New
Jersey logistics corridors in Elizabeth and Linden (across the Arthur Kill and
Kill Van Kull), and its distance numbers would look materially different if
those New Jersey lots were included.

**The 1-mile threshold.** A tract 1.1 miles from a lot is counted as a gap;
one at 0.9 miles is not. The distance-band table partially addresses this by
reporting continuous bands, but the binary classification in the maps and
borough table still uses the fixed threshold.

**Straight-line distance is not travel time.** Proximity to industrial land
says nothing about road network connectivity, traffic conditions, loading
access, or building entry -- the actual constraints that determine delivery
reach in an urban environment.

### What the Income Gradient Implies for Compensation

The core finding is that NYC census tracts closer to industrial land have
lower median household incomes, and the gradient is continuous and
statistically robust. This does not prove that industrial proximity *causes*
lower incomes -- the causal story runs in at least two directions. Industrial
corridors may have developed where land was already cheap (lower-income areas).
Or affluent neighborhoods may have used zoning to exclude industrial uses over
time, making the correlation partly a product of political power. Distinguishing
those explanations requires zoning history data this analysis does not include.

What the gradient does support is a revealed-preference reading: across 2,206
NYC tracts, proximity to industrial land is consistently associated with lower
household income. People with more resources tend to live farther from
industrial land. That is consistent with the Netherlands research finding a
15% property value discount within 250 meters of industrial sites. Both
patterns point in the same direction: industrial proximity is something people
pay to avoid, or are compensated for through lower housing costs.

**Back to the $10,000 question.** NorthPoint offered Hazle Township residents
$10,000 -- a one-time payment -- to accept a 1,300-acre data center. The
Netherlands study gives the clearest benchmark: homes within 250 meters of
industrial sites sold for roughly 15% less. If a home near the facility is
worth $200,000, that is a $30,000 loss in asset value -- a one-time hit,
three times the NorthPoint offer. The discount fades with distance, so homes
within 250 meters bear the full 15% while homes closer to 1.4 miles out see
almost nothing. A flat $10,000 to everyone ignores that geography entirely.

The NYC income gradient is a different signal -- correlation rather than cost.
Tracts within 0.25 miles of industrial land have a median income of $63,708;
tracts beyond 1 mile average $90,379. That $26,671 gap reflects sorting (more
affluent households can afford to live farther from industrial land) rather
than a direct price tag. Still, the direction is consistent: industrial
proximity is something people pay to avoid, or accept in exchange for lower
housing costs. A one-time $10,000 payment is a thin rationale for waiving
that for a generation-long facility.

None of this means developers should not build. It means the compensation
question deserves an empirical basis rather than an arbitrary one. Relevant
inputs would include: the distance from the facility to each household, local
property values before and after comparable facilities opened nearby, the
expected duration of impacts (noise, traffic, visual), and whether payments
should be structured as one-time checks or ongoing annual compensation tied
to the facility's operating life.

### Implications for the AI Data Center Debate

The current conflict over data center siting is partly a zoning fight, partly
an AI anxiety fight, and partly a legitimate dispute over who pays for
infrastructure that benefits a much larger population. In Q2 2026 alone,
community resistance stalled $68 billion in data center projects across the U.S.
[TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/22/everyone-can-find-a-reason-to-dislike-data-center-construction/)
reported in September 2026 that "everyone can find a reason to dislike data
center construction" -- from noise and water use to AI ethics to simple
neighborhood character concerns.

The [Brookings Institution](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/data-center-backlash-signals-a-fight-over-ai-power/)
framed it as a democratic accountability problem: if AI infrastructure is
built wherever companies choose, with local governments as the only check,
communities end up negotiating individually against well-resourced developers
without any common framework for what fair compensation looks like.

NYC's industrial land income gradient does not resolve that, but it offers
one empirical anchor. A city with 80 years of industrial zoning history shows
that people consistently sort away from industrial land when they can afford
to. If data center developers want to change that sorting -- to site facilities
in areas where residents have the resources to object and the political
influence to succeed -- the offer needs to reflect what those residents are
actually giving up.

Whether the comparison between NYC's industrial lots and a Pennsylvania data
center is close enough to matter is a judgment call. The land uses are
different. The geography is different. But the core mechanism -- a large
industrial facility arrives near a residential area, generating noise, traffic,
and visual impact -- is similar enough that the income gradient finding is at
minimum a reasonable order-of-magnitude reference, and significantly better
than no empirical basis at all.
