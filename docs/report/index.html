Introduction

Urban freight depends on industrial land. Warehouses, distribution centers, and freight terminals require large, accessible lots – the kind that are scarce and expensive in dense cities like New York.

This analysis maps how NYC’s industrial land is distributed relative to where people actually live. It asks: which census tracts fall entirely outside a 1-mile radius of any industrial lot, how many residents live in those tracts, and how does that pattern vary by borough and income?

To answer it, I combine 2020 Census population data at the census tract level with NYC’s PLUTO property database, which classifies every tax lot by land use. I filter for industrial and manufacturing lots (land use code 06) and buffer each lot by 1 mile. A census tract is classified as covered if any part of it intersects that buffer zone; tracts with no intersection are classified as industrial land gaps. Separately, I compute the straight-line distance from each tract centroid to its nearest industrial lot to enable continuous distance-band analysis beyond the binary classification.

What this measures and what it does not. According to the NYC DCP PLUTO Data Dictionary, land use code 06 maps to building classes E* (warehouses), F* (factories), and L* (loft buildings). The category is a reasonable proxy for freight-relevant industrial space, but it includes factories and loft buildings alongside distribution-oriented warehouses. Conversely, some last-mile delivery in Manhattan runs from microhubs and commercial spaces not classified as industrial in PLUTO. The results here are best read as a first-pass map of where industrial land exists relative to population, not a direct measure of delivery coverage.

The 1-mile threshold is intentionally tight. A 5-mile radius eliminates virtually all gaps in NYC. The tighter radius surfaces meaningful geographic variation across the five boroughs.

Data note (updated October 7, 2026): An earlier version of this report (published October 4) showed 38 gap tracts and 169,223 residents, with 14 Manhattan gap tracts. Those numbers were wrong. The Socrata API returns results in pages, and the initial pull hit the default 1,000-row limit, returning an incomplete PLUTO industrial lot dataset. A corrected pull (no row limit) retrieved the full set. With complete data, Manhattan has near-complete industrial land coverage (only 1 gap tract), and the city-wide gap falls to 31 tracts and 61,426 residents. All numbers in this version reflect the corrected pull.

A note on the industrial lot data: PLUTO classifies every NYC tax lot by land use code. This analysis filters for code 06 (Industrial & Manufacturing), which maps to building classes E* (warehouses), F (factories), and L (loft buildings). The category is broader than distribution-oriented warehouses alone: it includes factories and loft buildings, and gives no indication of whether a lot hosts an active logistics operation. These lots are used here as a publicly available proxy for industrial land; they are not a direct measure of delivery or logistics infrastructure.

Methodology

The analysis follows four steps:

1. Data acquisition. Census tract population and geometry are pulled from the 2020 Decennial Census via the tidycensus package. Borough boundaries come from the Census TIGER/Line files via tigris. Industrial lot locations are queried from the NYC PLUTO dataset via the Socrata JSON API with no row limit, then converted to point geometry using the lot centroid coordinates. Median household income is pulled from the 2020 ACS 5-year estimates.

2. Coordinate reference system. All layers are projected to EPSG:2263 (New York State Plane, Long Island), which uses feet as its unit of measurement. This CRS is optimized for accuracy in the New York metro area and is the standard used by NYC government GIS data.

3. Buffer analysis. Each industrial lot centroid is buffered by 5,280 feet (1 mile). The resulting circles are dissolved into a single merged coverage polygon using st_union(). This merged polygon represents the total area of NYC within 1 mile of at least one industrial lot.

4. Gap identification. Each census tract is tested for intersection with the coverage polygon using st_intersects(). Tracts with no intersection are classified as “far from industrial land.” In addition, the straight-line distance from each tract centroid to its nearest industrial lot is computed using st_distance(), enabling continuous distance-band analysis beyond the binary classification.

Distance-Band Analysis

The binary gap classification (covered vs. not) masks variation in how far residents actually are from industrial land. This section reports the share of population within each distance band, by borough, and the median straight-line distance from tract centroid to the nearest industrial lot.

Two distance measures are in use here and they give different numbers intentionally. The borough table (below) classifies a tract as “covered” if any part of it intersects a 1-mile buffer – the most generous possible reading. The distance table here uses the tract centroid, which is stricter: a large tract whose center sits 1.1 miles away counts as a gap by centroid even if its edge is technically within range. The 0.7% city-wide figure comes from the buffer method; the centroid method shows about 6% of Queens residents and 12% of Staten Island residents more than a mile from industrial land.

Separating geography from politics would require zoning history and land use change data over time.

Discussion

Key Findings

Queens has the largest gap population (32,622 residents across 13 tracts), followed by Brooklyn (19,809 across 11 tracts). Both sets of gap tracts sit at the outer edges of their boroughs, distant from the industrial corridors concentrated along the waterfront and freight routes closer to the urban core.

Manhattan has only one gap tract (GEOID 36061000100, Census Tract 1), with zero counted residents. Its centroid falls at approximately 40.695°N, 74.043°W, in the harbor southwest of the Battery – consistent with harbor islands (Governors Island, Ellis Island, or Liberty Island) that carry no permanent residents. Zero is most likely a true count, not a differential privacy artifact. Manhattan’s near-complete industrial land coverage otherwise reflects the density of lots in specific corridors (West Side railyards, Meatpacking District, Hudson Yards area).

Income data shows gap tracts skew higher-income than the rest of the city. This is consistent with the outer-borough position of those tracts, but the data cannot rule out zoning exclusion. The Manhattan gap tract has no income data, since it has no counted population.

The distance-band analysis adds resolution beyond the binary gap classification. About 16% of New Yorkers (96.6% within a mile minus 80.4% within half a mile) live in the 0.5–1 mile band. Staten Island stands out with the longest median tract distance (0.50 miles, vs. 0.21–0.31 for the other four boroughs). Queens has a shorter median (0.27 miles) but a longer right tail: about 6% of Queens residents are more than a mile from industrial land by the centroid measure, the second-highest share after Staten Island.

Limitations

Broad land use category. According to the NYC DCP PLUTO Data Dictionary, land use code 06 maps to building classes E* (warehouses), F* (factories), and L* (loft buildings). Factories and lofts are included alongside distribution-oriented warehouses, and the category gives no indication of whether a lot hosts an active logistics operation. This analysis measures proximity to industrial land as a class – it does not distinguish a 500,000 sq ft fulfillment center from a converted loft building or an inactive factory floor. A more operationally precise analysis would require commercial real estate data (CoStar, CBRE) or cross-referencing with business license records to isolate active logistics facilities.

Industrial land is not the only freight infrastructure. Real last-mile delivery in dense neighborhoods often runs from microhubs, retail backrooms, and parking garages classified as commercial in PLUTO. The gaps measured here may overstate true freight access limitations for some neighborhoods.

Edge effect. This analysis counts only NYC-classified lots. Major distribution hubs in New Jersey (roughly 1 mile from west Manhattan) and just outside the city line in Nassau and Westchester are excluded. This inflates apparent gaps in western Manhattan, Staten Island, and eastern Queens. The effect is largest for Staten Island: the borough sits adjacent to major New Jersey logistics corridors in Elizabeth and Linden (across the Arthur Kill and Kill Van Kull), and its distance numbers would look materially different if those New Jersey lots were included.

The 1-mile threshold. A tract 1.1 miles from a lot is counted as a gap; one at 0.9 miles is not. The distance-band table partially addresses this by reporting continuous bands, but the binary classification in the maps and borough table still uses the fixed threshold.

Straight-line distance is not travel time. Proximity to industrial land says nothing about road network connectivity, traffic conditions, loading access, or building entry – the actual constraints that determine delivery reach in an urban environment.

Implications

This analysis is a map of where NYC’s industrial land sits relative to its population, not a map of where deliveries can or cannot reach. What it does show is meaningful: the uneven geographic distribution of industrial land across the five boroughs, the income pattern of tracts farthest from that land, and the particular position of Staten Island and outer Queens relative to the industrial corridors concentrated near the waterfront.

For logistics planners, the relevant next step is not “where should a fulfillment center go” – that requires demand data, road network travel times, rent levels, and zoning analysis this dataset cannot provide. The relevant question is narrower: which of the gap tracts identified here are actually near commercial-grade delivery infrastructure that PLUTO doesn’t capture? Answering that requires either commercial data or field validation.

For urban planners and policy researchers, the income pattern is worth following up: the tracts farthest from industrial land skew higher-income, which is consistent with either deliberate exclusion of industrial uses from affluent neighborhoods or simply with the geography of where the city’s industrial corridors developed historically. Distinguishing between those explanations would require zoning history and land use change data over time.

## Introduction

Urban freight depends on industrial land. Warehouses, distribution centers,
and freight terminals require large, accessible lots -- the kind that are
scarce and expensive in dense cities like New York.

This analysis maps how NYC's industrial land is distributed relative to where
people actually live. It asks: **which census tracts fall entirely outside a
1-mile radius of any industrial lot, how many residents live in those tracts,
and how does that pattern vary by borough and income?**

To answer it, I combine 2020 Census population data at the census tract level
with NYC's PLUTO property database, which classifies every tax lot by land
use. I filter for industrial and manufacturing lots (land use code 06) and
buffer each lot by 1 mile. A census tract is classified as covered if any
part of it intersects that buffer zone; tracts with no intersection are
classified as industrial land gaps. Separately, I compute the straight-line
distance from each tract centroid to its nearest industrial lot to enable
continuous distance-band analysis beyond the binary classification.

**What this measures and what it does not.** According to the NYC DCP PLUTO
Data Dictionary, land use code 06 maps to building classes E* (warehouses),
F* (factories), and L* (loft buildings). The category is a reasonable proxy
for freight-relevant industrial space, but it includes factories and loft
buildings alongside distribution-oriented warehouses. Conversely, some
last-mile delivery in Manhattan runs from microhubs and commercial spaces not
classified as industrial in PLUTO. The results here are best read as a
first-pass map of where industrial land exists relative to population, not a
direct measure of delivery coverage.

The 1-mile threshold is intentionally tight. A 5-mile radius eliminates
virtually all gaps in NYC. The tighter radius surfaces meaningful geographic
variation across the five boroughs.

> row limit) retrieved the full set. With complete data, Manhattan has
> near-complete industrial land coverage (only 1 gap tract), and the city-wide
> gap falls to 31 tracts and 61,426 residents. All numbers in this version
> reflect the corrected pull.

## Data Sources

All data is pulled programmatically via public APIs. No manual file downloads
are required to reproduce this analysis.

| Dataset | Source | Year | Access Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Census tract boundaries + population | U.S. Census Bureau (Decennial Census) | 2020 | `tidycensus` R package |
| Borough boundaries | U.S. Census Bureau (TIGER/Line) | 2024 | `tigris` R package |
| Industrial/warehouse tax lots | NYC Dept. of City Planning — PLUTO | Current | NYC Open Data Socrata API |
| Median household income by tract | U.S. Census Bureau (ACS 5-year) | 2020 | `tidycensus` R package |

**A note on the industrial lot data:** PLUTO classifies every NYC tax lot by
land use code. This analysis filters for code `06` (Industrial &
Manufacturing), which maps to building classes E* (warehouses), F*
(factories), and L* (loft buildings). The category is broader than
distribution-oriented warehouses alone: it includes factories and loft
buildings, and gives no indication of whether a lot hosts an active logistics
operation. These lots are used here as a publicly available proxy for
industrial land; they are not a direct measure of delivery or logistics
infrastructure.

---

## Methodology

The analysis follows four steps:

**1. Data acquisition.** Census tract population and geometry are pulled from
the 2020 Decennial Census via the `tidycensus` package. Borough boundaries
come from the Census TIGER/Line files via `tigris`. Industrial lot locations
are queried from the NYC PLUTO dataset via the Socrata JSON API with no row
limit, then converted to point geometry using the lot centroid coordinates.
Median household income is pulled from the 2020 ACS 5-year estimates.

**2. Coordinate reference system.** All layers are projected to EPSG:2263
(New York State Plane, Long Island), which uses feet as its unit of
measurement. This CRS is optimized for accuracy in the New York metro area
and is the standard used by NYC government GIS data.

**3. Buffer analysis.** Each industrial lot centroid is buffered by 5,280
feet (1 mile). The resulting circles are dissolved into a single merged
coverage polygon using `st_union()`. This merged polygon represents the
total area of NYC within 1 mile of at least one industrial lot.

**4. Gap identification.** Each census tract is tested for intersection with
the coverage polygon using `st_intersects()`. Tracts with no intersection
are classified as "far from industrial land." In addition, the straight-line
distance from each tract centroid to its nearest industrial lot is computed
using `st_distance()`, enabling continuous distance-band analysis beyond the
binary classification.

### Distance-Band Analysis

The binary gap classification (covered vs. not) masks variation in how far
residents actually are from industrial land. This section reports the share
of population within each distance band, by borough, and the median straight-line
distance from tract centroid to the nearest industrial lot.

Two distance measures are in use here and they give different numbers
intentionally. The borough table (below) classifies a tract as "covered" if
any part of it intersects a 1-mile buffer -- the most generous possible
reading. The distance table here uses the tract centroid, which is stricter:
a large tract whose center sits 1.1 miles away counts as a gap by centroid
even if its edge is technically within range. The 0.7% city-wide figure
comes from the buffer method; the centroid method shows about 6% of Queens
residents and 12% of Staten Island residents more than a mile from industrial
land.

## Discussion

### Key Findings

Queens has the largest gap population (32,622 residents across 13 tracts),
followed by Brooklyn (19,809 across 11 tracts). Both sets of gap tracts sit at
the outer edges of their boroughs, distant from the industrial corridors
concentrated along the waterfront and freight routes closer to the urban core.

Manhattan has only one gap tract (GEOID 36061000100, Census Tract 1), with
zero counted residents. Its centroid falls at approximately 40.695°N, 74.043°W,
in the harbor southwest of the Battery -- consistent with harbor islands
(Governors Island, Ellis Island, or Liberty Island) that carry no permanent
residents. Zero is most likely a true count, not a differential privacy
artifact. Manhattan's near-complete industrial land coverage otherwise reflects
the density of lots in specific corridors (West Side railyards, Meatpacking
District, Hudson Yards area).

Income data shows gap tracts skew higher-income than the rest of the city. This
is consistent with the outer-borough position of those tracts, but the data
cannot rule out zoning exclusion. The Manhattan gap tract has no income data,
since it has no counted population.

The distance-band analysis adds resolution beyond the binary gap classification.
About 16% of New Yorkers (96.6% within a mile minus 80.4% within half a mile)
live in the 0.5--1 mile band. Staten Island stands out with the longest median
tract distance (0.50 miles, vs. 0.21--0.31 for the other four boroughs).
Queens has a shorter median (0.27 miles) but a longer right tail: about 6% of
Queens residents are more than a mile from industrial land by the centroid
measure, the second-highest share after Staten Island.

### Limitations

**Broad land use category.** According to the NYC DCP PLUTO Data Dictionary,
land use code 06 maps to building classes E* (warehouses), F* (factories), and
L* (loft buildings). Factories and lofts are included alongside
distribution-oriented warehouses, and the category gives no indication of
whether a lot hosts an active logistics operation. This analysis measures
proximity to industrial land as a class -- it does not distinguish a 500,000 sq
ft fulfillment center from a converted loft building or an inactive factory
floor. A more operationally precise analysis would require commercial real
estate data (CoStar, CBRE) or cross-referencing with business license records
to isolate active logistics facilities.

**Industrial land is not the only freight infrastructure.** Real last-mile
delivery in dense neighborhoods often runs from microhubs, retail backrooms,
and parking garages classified as commercial in PLUTO. The gaps measured here
may overstate true freight access limitations for some neighborhoods.

**Edge effect.** This analysis counts only NYC-classified lots. Major
distribution hubs in New Jersey (roughly 1 mile from west Manhattan) and just
outside the city line in Nassau and Westchester are excluded. This inflates
apparent gaps in western Manhattan, Staten Island, and eastern Queens. The
effect is largest for Staten Island: the borough sits adjacent to major New
Jersey logistics corridors in Elizabeth and Linden (across the Arthur Kill and
Kill Van Kull), and its distance numbers would look materially different if
those New Jersey lots were included.

**The 1-mile threshold.** A tract 1.1 miles from a lot is counted as a gap;
one at 0.9 miles is not. The distance-band table partially addresses this by
reporting continuous bands, but the binary classification in the maps and
borough table still uses the fixed threshold.

**Straight-line distance is not travel time.** Proximity to industrial land
says nothing about road network connectivity, traffic conditions, loading
access, or building entry -- the actual constraints that determine delivery
reach in an urban environment.

### Implications

This analysis is a map of where NYC's industrial land sits relative to its
population, not a map of where deliveries can or cannot reach. What it does
show is meaningful: the uneven geographic distribution of industrial land
across the five boroughs, the income pattern of tracts farthest from that
land, and the particular position of Staten Island and outer Queens relative
to the industrial corridors concentrated near the waterfront.

For logistics planners, the relevant next step is not "where should a
fulfillment center go" -- that requires demand data, road network travel
times, rent levels, and zoning analysis this dataset cannot provide. The
relevant question is narrower: which of the gap tracts identified here are
actually near commercial-grade delivery infrastructure that PLUTO doesn't
capture? Answering that requires either commercial data or field validation.

For urban planners and policy researchers, the income pattern is worth
following up: the tracts farthest from industrial land skew higher-income,
which is consistent with either deliberate exclusion of industrial uses from
affluent neighborhoods or simply with the geography of where the city's
industrial corridors developed historically. Distinguishing between those
explanations would require zoning history and land use change data over time.
